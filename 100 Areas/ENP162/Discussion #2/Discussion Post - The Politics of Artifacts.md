## Response:
Langdon Winner's article, "Do Artifacts Have Politics?" argues that technology is not neutral and embodies political values. He identifies two ways artifacts can be political: instances in which the invention or design becomes a way of settling an issue in a particular kinds of political relationships or when a man-made system appear require or emulate political relationships. Winner uses Robert Moses’ urban planning to illustrate this, showing how overpasses were intentionally built too low for buses, restricting lower-income and racial minority communities. This example highlights how biases in technological design can become normalized over time, shaping societal structures without explicit recognition.

Winner’s arguments are increasingly relevant in today’s digital landscape, where algorithmic decision-making systems reinforce systemic biases. Ruha Benjamin’s article, _Assessing Risk, Automating Racism_ published, examines how automated systems amplify racial discrimination. These systems, often presented as objective, enforce historical biases into predictive models, disproportionately impacting marginalized communities. This is true whether or not it is the intention of the designer as it is something taught throughout the system and permeates through data collection. Social media platforms like Facebook manipulate user behavior to maximize engagement and profit, demonstrating how digital systems prioritize corporate interests over societal well-being. These technologies, under the guise of neutrality, actively shape power dynamics and deepen inequalities.
