There were multiple human factors at play in the Air France 447 and Lion Air 610 crashes. In both cases, loss of situational awareness was a critical issue, as the pilots became confused about their aircraft's status and failed to recognize they were in a stall. Improper training also played a major role, particularly in dealing with high-altitude stalls, manual flying at cruise altitude, and handling automated systems like MCAS. Fatigue was a factor for the Air France captain, who had gotten minimal sleep. Poor crew resource management and communication breakdowns between pilots were evident in both incidents. There was also an overreliance on automation, with pilots unaccustomed to manually flying their aircraft in challenging situation. The Lion Air pilots specifically lacked deep "airmanship" skills beyond rote procedures.

The automation systems themselves contributed significantly to the accidents. There was insufficient communication of system status to the pilots, particularly regarding the MCAS system on the 737 MAX. Technical failures occurred with the pitot tubes on AF447 and the angle of attack sensor on the Lion Air flight. A lack of failure tolerance was present, with the loss of airspeed data causing major issues on AF447 and MCAS relying on a single sensor. The flight deck designs, while meant to improve safety, in some ways hindered shared situational awareness between pilots. Environmental factors like nighttime conditions and storms played a role in AF447, while Lion Air 610 occurred in daytime clear weather. Ultimately, the interplay between increasingly automated aircraft and pilots with diminishing manual flying skills created a dangerous scenario that these crews were unable to successfully resolve.

It is also important to note the systemic problems involved in the airline business. With airplanes becoming safer and easier to fly, it was more accessible to own an airline and they boomed. At the same time, the business on  both the part of the airliner and manufacturer incentivized cost cutting and doing everything possible to keep planes in the air. This ultimately withered away important safety norms that should be held at all points of the aviation industry.
