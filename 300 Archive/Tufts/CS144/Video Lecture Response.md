**a short summary of the research such that someone who hasn't seen the talk and does not know anything about the speaker can still get a sense of what the research is about**
Anastasia Ostrowski presents her paper Equitable Co-Design which highlights the inherently biased implementation of robots by roboticists, and how these humanistic flaws embedded in the robot's design are deployed in places where they would have significant human affect. In contrast, there will also be groups of people that have no contact with robots and will experience a "Robot Divide" where they will not see the benefits of advancements in robotics. Ostrowski poses the question: "How do we support equitable design processes in robotics?" The study proposes that the answer lie within the ecosystem involving the roboticist, stakeholders, and policy makers. By facilitating more interaction between these agents, there will be engagement with historical, cultural, and societal. 
The ideas proposed are: Co-design and participatory design. The idea is that long-term design experience involving the user group would help to contextualize the roboticists design choices and give agency to the user group.
The study details a year-long co-design framework involving older adults as active contributors to social robot design. This framework integrates divergent and convergent activities, such as art-based image-making, robot prototyping, and lived experiences with robots. Participants were encouraged to explore personal needs and preferences, reflecting on interactions with a robot over time. The process prioritizes relationship-building, iterative feedback, and ethical considerations, producing actionable guidelines for user-centered robot design tailored to diverse user groups. 
Within the question and answer section, Ostowski explains further about how this framework can be applied in a varierty of industries, but also within education as future engineers are being educated and nurtured. It s also important to mention that this framework is general enough to be used within already made robots and studies.
While the study makes well thoughtout arguments, I would like to see this being applied and conducted while working with designers who are not necessarily already convinced that this frame work is useful. Really, I'm not convinced that the moral approach will be able to convince many roboticists to use this. Perhaps a future study involving designers who choose to adopt this framework and compares their experience and how effective they think the frame work is compared to designers who were not aware of this. 

**what is the long term vision of the researcher? Where do you see this research going in 5 years?**
The next steps for this research seem to be exploring the functionality and effectivity of this work in an industry setting such as health care. This also includes going to universities and exploring how this framework can be used in education for young professionals to build these skills. I also see this research expanding into different cultural contexts. This framework might be difficult to apply in communities that are more private about their lifestyle, beliefs, and may have general cultural differences. Extra work would have to be done on the side of the researchers to truly understand the users and allow it to remold the framework into a form they can use that would be respectable to the user group. 
**what are some open question that the researcher posed that remain to be addressed?**
Ostrowski touches on the need for validation of guidelines throughout the talk. Future work needs to be conducted to evaluate the practical effectivity of this framework by applying it to multiple real world scenarios in industry. There are also questions about user-group scalability and timeline flexibility, which could possibly be addressed in a future study like the one mentioned above.
