### Basis of Investigation
Research has shown that language-capable robots:
1. have significant **persuasive power** and can influence, persuade, and coerce humans in a variety of way
2. can **influence** interactants’ endorsements of **social and moral norms**
3. may have a **long-term impact on interactants’** social and moral behaviors and on the behaviors those interactants choose to condone or sanction in others

Thus, language-capable social robots, they must have the requisite moral competence to avoid damaging their social and moral ecosystem

### The Advisor Decider Relation

>[!tldr] Moral Disagreement Hypothesis
> People will more strongly blame an advisor who recommends the choice that they had re-jected, compared to an advisor who recommends the choice that they had endorsed.
<!-- element style="width:40%"-->


Psychological literature shows that real world advisee advisor relationships tend to take the safe approach in terms of risk aversion and rationality. This same prior literature leads to the baseline hypothesis above

Beyond evaluating this baseline hypothesis, the main aim of our experiments is to investigate whether human and robot morals advisors are blamed differently, both when they advise a human decider (in Experiment 1) and when they advise either a human or a robot decider (in Experiment 2). We develop hypotheses about these moral evaluations next.

---
### Experiment 1

>[!tldr] Advice as norm endorsement hypothesis:
> If people treat moral advice as an endorsement of a norm, then human and robot advisors should be blamed the same amount, because norms for dilemmas appear to be the same for humans and robots
<!-- element style="width:40%"-->


---
### Experiment 2
>[!tldr] Advice as decision hypothesis:
> If people treat moral advice more like a decision in which the advisor should be treated as if they were a decider, then human and robot advisors should be blamed differently, just like human and robot deciders have been blamed differently in previous research on moral dilemmas. Specifically, these studies found that robots were blamed more than humans for choosing inaction, so we expect that robot advisors, too, will be blamed more for recommending inaction.
<!-- element style="width:40%"-->

### Experiment 1 Results & Discussion:
- Normative expectations were applied equally to both the robot advisor and the human advisor, which further supports previous studies where that same thing can be applied to deciders.
- Evidence supports moral disagreement hypothesis: People assign higher blame to an advisor that goes against what they themselves would choose
- Evidence goes against advice as norm endorsement hypothesis: Robots were blamed more than human advisors
- Evidence supports advice as decision hypothesis: People blamed robots more for inaction

### Experiment 2 Setup:
- Experiment 2 also tests the possibility that there is less blame on the human for their decision because of the stress of the situation by testing
	- Human, robot decider
	- Human, robot advisor
- Overall this experiment retests experiment one while providing further analysis by expanding possibilities
	- Action, in-action for decider
	- Action, in-action for advisor
1. Replicate experiment 1 finding: human-robot in-action asymmetry for advise when the advisor recommends in-action and the decider is human
2. Human-robot inaction asymmetry for advice is larger when the decider is human than when the decider is a robot
3. Replication
4. New hypothesis: Penalty of dismissal hypothesis
	1. People will blame those deciders who dismiss the advice they received (whether action or inaction) more than deciders who follow it.
